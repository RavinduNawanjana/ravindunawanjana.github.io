Research / Statements / Financing resilient water systems

Research statement for the PhD programme in Environmental Economics, Yale School of the Environment, Fall 2027. Written with reference to the work of Professor David Keiser.

My MSc dissertation at London Metropolitan University asked why climate finance reaches so few of the places that need it. It ended with a theoretical contribution I call the Institutional Reinforcement Cycle (IRC). I want to spend my doctoral years at Yale turning the IRC from a framework I believe in into something a skeptical economist would accept. That means writing it down formally, estimating it, and finding out where it fails.

The IRC treats a climate finance system as a product of its components rather than a sum. Institutional collaboration, policy alignment, blended finance deployment and transparency compound, so a value near zero on one of them can stall the whole system however much capital sits in the funds. My evidence so far is a survey of 103 climate finance professionals and eight interviews with practitioners at UNDP, UNCDF, UN Sustainable Stock Exchanges, FAO, the African Development Bank, the Asian Development Bank, the World Bank GEF, a central bank and a private capital firm. It points to institutional collaboration, rather than the volume of blended finance, as the binding constraint. I presented this at the 3rd International Conference on Biomimicry for Sustainability in August 2026, and the paper is now under revision at Climate and Development. A survey of this size motivates the multiplicative form but does not identify it, and getting to an estimable, identified specification is the first thing I would like to work on with you.

In a recent Wall Street Journal interview on the Colorado River, the water law scholar Rhett Larson put the problem in one line. "We are not running out of water. We are running out of cheap water." Scarcity there is economic, a question of what it costs to move, store and clean water, and the crisis grew partly from allocations set in unusually wet years. In Small Island Developing States and Least Developed Countries the same logic applies with a sharper edge. Desalination, storage, treatment and piped networks are capital-intensive assets with long lives, so the price of water there is in large part the price of capital. The direction I most want to pursue with you, Professor Keiser, is how to finance resilient water systems in these economies. My layered capital structure has a concessional first loss tranche, a multilateral guarantee mezzanine and a household deposit senior tranche. Applied to water, the question is whether it lowers the financing cost of a utility network or a community storage system enough to change what gets built, and whether the benefits exceed what the grants cost.

Your research is the closest evidence I have found on that last question. Your Quarterly Journal of Economics paper with Joseph Shapiro finds that Clean Water Act grants to municipal wastewater treatment reduced pollution, but that their estimated effects on housing values were smaller than their costs. The PNAS paper with Catherine Kling and Joseph Shapiro describes the measured benefits of US water quality policy as low but uncertain. Water infrastructure in poorer economies faces the same test with thinner data and costlier capital. Three other strands map onto the IRC. The study of drinking water earmarks asks who public water money actually reaches, which is the accountability question at the center of my framework. The work in progress with Yingyi Jin on water system consolidation speaks to its collaboration term, since small, fragmented systems are exactly the institutions that struggle to borrow. The paper on lead monitoring, which detects manipulation of reported results near a regulatory threshold and finds that it largely disappeared after the Flint crisis, is the transparency component seen from the regulator's side.

Across these papers the tools stay constant while the settings change. Quasi-experimental econometrics identifies what a policy did, and integrated assessment models translate physical change into social costs and benefits, as in your Annual Review of Resource Economics article with Nicholas Muller on integrated assessment models for air and water, and in the Social Cost of Water Pollution workshop you co-founded. Those are the tools I want to build my doctoral work on. My preparation so far is applied. My MSc analyzed a 103-respondent survey through reliability analysis, correlation and multiple regression with diagnostics. I ran a difference in differences additionality test with the Ministry of Environment and Ministry of Agriculture in Sri Lanka. I have priced an ESG-linked option embedded in a green structured note using Black-Scholes, and I want to extend that valuation to incomplete markets. I work in R and Quarto, with all analysis code public on GitHub, and I am building fluency in Python and Stata. The first year theory and econometrics sequence, followed by field courses in econometrics and environmental valuation, is where I intend to turn this into the formal training the work needs.

A second direction connects the IRC to water integrated assessment. Water IAMs value changes in quality and quantity, but they rarely ask how the cost of capital shapes which improvements get financed at all. In developing economies that cost is partly an outcome of institutional architecture, which is what the IRC tries to capture. I would like to estimate that relationship from data and add it to a water IAM as a regional wedge in the cost of capital. I would then ask how much of the gap in water security between SIDS, LDCs and developed economies traces back to institutions rather than hydrology. The work in progress on drought adaptation in Brazil, and your earlier consulting for the UNDP Pacific Adaptation to Climate Change Project, point to natural settings in which to test it.

What I bring beyond methods is field experience. In 2025 I wrote Sri Lanka's expression of interest to the Climate Investment Funds' Industry Decarbonization Program, so I have seen from the inside how concessional finance is designed and allocated in a developing economy. I have also sat across the table from rice farmers in Sri Lanka's dry zone while we negotiated an agricultural carbon credit agreement. My feasibility study for that program examined alternate wetting and drying, which cuts methane and also reduces the irrigation water a paddy needs, so a carbon credit there is partly a water security instrument. Before that meeting I had run the additionality test myself with the government, a difference in differences comparison of adopters and nonadopters of the proposed practice changes, checking whether each intervention exceeded VM0042's 5 percent quantitative change threshold and whether regional adoption stayed below the 20 percent common practice ceiling. The farmers were not worried about methodology. They wanted to know what happens to their land rights if the project outlasts the company that signed it. Larson describes a similar tension in Australia's Murray-Darling reform, where tradable water shares replaced fixed allocations but had to be regulated so that speculators could not buy them up and hoard them. Who holds a right, and who backs it when an institution fails, is a moral hazard and enforcement problem inside blended finance, and I want the tools to model it rather than describe it.

To keep the work tied to data, I plan to run two small feasibility pilots with commercial banks during the doctorate, one in a Small Island Developing State and one in a Least Developed Country, and water infrastructure is a natural first use. My relationships with Dr. Pradeep Kurukulasuriya at UNCDF, Jason Spensley at the World Bank GEF and Dr. Anthony Miller at UN Sustainable Stock Exchanges make this possible without large seed capital. Their feedback on my early model complicated it usefully. Mr. Spensley, for one, thought the mechanism technically feasible but politically uncertain. The pilot's purpose is to produce real pricing, take-up and default data under a guarantee. I could then use that data to discipline the model's parameters and see where the structure breaks.

This work sits inside a wider program, the capital structure itself and its applications, which my personal statement describes. I have attended three UNFCCC COPs and two UNCCD COPs, and in those rooms the gap between capital pledged and capital deployed was the most consistent problem I saw. Water is where that gap is felt most directly, and where I think the empirical and valuation discipline of your group, Professor Keiser, would do the most for this work. In the longer run, I want to build this agenda as an academic, with a research group that studies how finance reaches developing economies and stays close to the institutions that deploy it.
